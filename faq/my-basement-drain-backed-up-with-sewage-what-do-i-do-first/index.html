Sewage & Contaminated Water Cleanup

My basement drain backed up with sewage — what do I do first?

Quick Answer:

Stop using water immediately, leave the basement, call a licensed Category 3 restoration contractor, and don't attempt cleanup yourself.

Key Takeaways

  • Sewage backup is IICRC Category 3 (black water) — the highest contamination classification, containing bacteria, viruses, and parasites that pose serious health risks.
  • Mold can begin growing on wet organic materials within 24–48 hours per IICRC S520, so time from first call to extraction matters.
  • Standard homeowners insurance typically does NOT cover sewer backup unless you've added a sewer/water backup endorsement — check your policy before assuming coverage.
  • Peoria's older housing stock — especially pre-1980 homes in Peoria Heights with aging cast iron drain lines — is especially vulnerable to drain backups during heavy rain and spring snowmelt.

Why Is a Sewage Backup in Your Basement an Emergency?

A basement drain that backs up with sewage isn't a plumbing inconvenience — it's a biohazard event. Under the IICRC S500 standard, sewage-contaminated water is classified as Category 3, the most serious contamination level. Category 3 water contains bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites capable of causing serious illness on contact — including through skin, inhalation of dried aerosols, and accidental ingestion.

Raw sewage backup is different from a burst pipe or appliance overflow. Where a clean-water (Category 1) loss from a supply line primarily threatens your structure, a sewage backup threatens both your structure and anyone who enters the space. Everything the water touched — drywall, framing, carpet, personal belongings, concrete block — is now considered contaminated until proven otherwise by a certified restoration technician.

If you've already been in the space before reading this, wash your hands and any exposed skin thoroughly with soap and water. If sewage contacted your eyes or mouth, seek medical attention. Then come back here.

⚠️ Watch out: Do not run any water in the house — dishwasher, washing machine, toilets, sinks — until a plumber confirms the drain line blockage is cleared. Running water will push more sewage into your basement and increase the scope of contamination significantly.

What Do I Do in the First 30 Minutes?

The first half hour after a sewage backup determines a lot about how bad the final bill will be. Here's what to do, in order:

  1. Get everyone out of the basement immediately. Children, pets, anyone with a compromised immune system especially. Category 3 contamination is not a situation where you assess damage while standing in it.
  2. Turn off electricity to the basement if you can do so safely from outside the affected area. If the electrical panel is in the basement and already wet, call an electrician before entering. Water and live circuits are a lethal combination.
  3. Do NOT run any water in the house. This includes flushing toilets. Every gallon that goes down a drain can back up into your basement if the line is still blocked.
  4. Call a licensed plumber to clear the blockage AND a Category 3 certified restoration contractor at the same time. These are two separate calls. The plumber fixes the source. The restoration contractor handles the contaminated water, materials, and structural drying.
  5. Call your insurance company or agent to report the loss. Do this before cleanup begins — insurers want documentation of pre-remediation conditions. Photographs and video taken before extraction begins are your evidence.
  6. Document everything with your phone. Wide shots and close-ups. Water level marks on walls. Affected belongings. Damage to flooring and drywall. This documentation is critical for your insurance adjuster.
💡 Tip: Peoria homeowners covered by Pekin Insurance, Country Financial, or State Farm should note that local adjusters in the Peoria–Pekin market are familiar with regional restoration pricing and the cost ranges associated with Category 3 losses. Accurate pre-remediation documentation from a certified contractor using Xactimate line items gives your claim the clearest possible paper trail.

What Will the Restoration Contractor Actually Do?

When the IICRC-certified contractor we connect you with arrives, they won't just start vacuuming up water. Category 3 restoration follows a specific protocol under IICRC S500 — and for good reason. Any shortcut on contaminated water creates liability, long-term structural damage, and ongoing health risk.

Assessment and containment

The team first identifies the extent of contamination using moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras. Sewage doesn't stay where you see it — it wicks up drywall, saturates subfloor, and migrates into wall cavities. Areas affected by Category 3 water are typically contained to prevent cross-contamination to unaffected parts of the home.

Extraction of standing water and solids

Truck-mounted extraction equipment removes standing sewage. This is not a wet-vac job. Category 3 extraction requires equipment rated for contaminated water and technicians in appropriate PPE — gloves, N95 or higher respirators, eye protection, disposable coveralls.

Removal of contaminated materials

Unlike Category 1 water damage, Category 3 losses almost always require removal of porous materials that absorbed sewage — drywall, carpet, carpet pad, insulation, and sometimes OSB subfloor or wood framing. The IICRC S500 standard does not allow porous materials contaminated by Category 3 water to be dried in place and left. They come out. This is non-negotiable from both a health and an IICRC-compliance standpoint.

Antimicrobial treatment and structural drying

After affected materials are removed, remaining structural surfaces are treated with EPA-registered antimicrobial agents. Then commercial-grade air movers and dehumidifiers are deployed to bring the structure to IICRC drying targets. Technicians take daily moisture readings and log them — this data becomes part of your insurance documentation and proves the job was completed to standard.

Category 3 Sewage Cleanup: What the Process Covers

  • Sewage extraction using commercial-grade equipment rated for contaminated water
  • Removal of all porous materials that absorbed Category 3 water (per IICRC S500)
  • EPA-registered antimicrobial treatment of exposed structural surfaces
  • Commercial structural drying with daily moisture logging
  • Mold prevention protocols per IICRC S520 (mold can begin in 24–48 hours on wet organics)
  • Written Xactimate documentation for insurance adjuster submission

Will My Homeowners Insurance Cover a Sewage Backup in Peoria?

This is the question everyone asks, and the honest answer is: it depends entirely on your policy. Standard homeowners insurance policies in Illinois — including those issued by Pekin Insurance, Country Financial, and State Farm — typically do not include sewer and water backup coverage as a default. It is usually available as an endorsement (add-on) for an additional premium, and many homeowners don't realize they never purchased it.

That said, there are situations where a backup-related loss may have coverage implications under the base policy — for example, if the backup was caused by a sudden and accidental failure of an internal drain component rather than a municipal sewer issue. Do not make coverage assumptions before talking to your adjuster. What you can control is the documentation: call your insurer immediately, do not throw anything away before it's documented, and make sure your restoration contractor provides a full written scope of work.

Peoria-area homeowners in Peoria Heights, Chillicothe, and other neighborhoods with older housing stock and aging cast iron drain lines are particularly at risk for sewer backup events during heavy spring rains — when the municipal system is at capacity and water finds the path of least resistance back into your basement. If you've had one backup, it's worth a conversation with your agent about adding the endorsement before the next one happens.

⚠️ Watch out: Do not begin throwing away contaminated items — furniture, rugs, stored belongings — before your adjuster has authorized the loss or a contractor has documented them. Discarding items before documentation can create gaps in your claim that are difficult to recover.

Could Mold Be a Problem After a Sewage Backup?

Yes — and it can start fast. The IICRC S520 mold remediation standard notes that mold can begin colonizing wet organic materials in as little as 24–48 hours under the right conditions. A Peoria basement in spring or summer — warm, humid, poorly ventilated — provides those conditions readily.

This is one reason why sewage backup cleanup cannot wait until Monday morning if it happens on a Friday night. Every hour of delay on Category 3 extraction is an hour closer to secondary mold growth on framing, subfloor, and anything else organic that stayed wet.

The good news: mold growth following sewage backup is not inevitable if extraction and drying begin promptly. The contractors we connect you with follow IICRC S520 protocols to prevent secondary mold colonization — including controlling temperature and humidity during the drying phase, applying antimicrobial agents to exposed framing, and monitoring conditions with daily moisture logs. If mold is already present on arrival, it will be identified and remediated as a separate scope item.

For more on what mold remediation involves and when it's required after water damage, the EPA's mold remediation guidance is a reliable starting point. The IICRC's S520 standard governs the professional remediation process used by certified contractors.

What Does Sewage Backup Cleanup Cost in the Peoria Area?

Category 3 sewage cleanup is among the most expensive categories of residential water damage restoration — and for good reason. The contamination protocols, PPE requirements, mandatory material removal, and extended drying cycles all add cost compared to a clean-water pipe burst.

In the Peoria market, a sewage backup cleanup in a typical residential basement can typically range from $4,000 to $15,000 or more depending on the volume of sewage, the square footage affected, how much finished material needs removal, and whether mold remediation is required as a second phase. A finished basement with drywall, carpet, and stored belongings generally represents a higher-cost loss than an unfinished utility basement with concrete walls. Your contractor's written estimate will reflect the specific conditions of your loss.

Under the Illinois Home Repair and Remodeling Act (HRRA), any contractor performing restoration work over $1,000 is required to provide you with a written contract before work begins. If a contractor shows up after a sewage backup and wants to start immediately without giving you a written scope — walk away. That written contract protects you and is also documentation your adjuster will want to see.

Get everyone out, stop running water, call a licensed plumber to clear the line and a certified restoration contractor at the same time, and document everything before cleanup begins.
