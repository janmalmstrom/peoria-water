Water Extraction & Structural Drying

What equipment is used to dry out a house after water damage?

By Marcus Webb

Quick Answer:

Professional water damage drying uses industrial air movers, refrigerant or desiccant dehumidifiers, moisture meters, thermal imaging cameras, and negative air machines — far more capable than any consumer hardware store rental.

Key Takeaways

  • Industrial air movers move 1,500–3,000 CFM of air — roughly 10–15x more than a household fan — accelerating evaporation from wet structural materials.
  • Desiccant dehumidifiers outperform refrigerant units in cold conditions, making them the preferred choice during Peoria's January–February freeze events when unheated spaces drop below 40°F.
  • IICRC S500 requires contractors to document moisture readings daily — without calibrated meters and thermal imaging, wet pockets inside walls go undetected until mold begins.
  • Negative air machines with HEPA filtration are required on Category 3 sewage backup jobs to prevent cross-contamination of dry areas of the home.

Why does professional drying equipment matter — and why can't you just use fans?

After a pipe bursts in a Peoria Heights bungalow or a sump pump fails in a Dunlap finished basement, the first instinct most homeowners have is to grab every box fan in the house and point them at the wet carpet. It's understandable — and it's not enough.

Household fans move air, but they don't remove moisture from it. Without a dehumidifier pulling water vapor out of the air and exhausting it, you're essentially moving humid air in circles. Wet framing, subfloor, and drywall keep off-gassing moisture back into the room. Relative humidity stays high. Drying stalls. And in Peoria's older housing stock — where many homes were built before 1980 with wood framing, plaster walls, and cast-iron drain lines — hidden moisture in wall cavities is exactly where mold takes hold.

The IICRC S500 Standard for Professional Water Damage Restoration sets the benchmark for how structural drying should be performed, including the equipment required, placement protocols, and daily documentation standards. Contractors connected through our network follow S500 — which means calibrated equipment, specific air mover and dehumidifier ratios, and moisture logs that hold up with State Farm, Pekin Insurance, and Country Financial adjusters.

Core Equipment Used in Professional Structural Drying

  • Industrial air movers (axial and centrifugal)
  • Refrigerant dehumidifiers (standard interior conditions)
  • Desiccant dehumidifiers (cold spaces, high humidity loads)
  • Thermal imaging cameras (infrared)
  • Penetrating and non-penetrating moisture meters
  • Thermo-hygrometers (air temperature and relative humidity)
  • Negative air machines with HEPA filtration
  • Injectidry systems and wall cavity drying panels
  • Truck-mounted or portable water extractors

Air movers: what they are and how they're deployed

An industrial air mover — sometimes called a snail blower or axial fan depending on its design — is the workhorse of structural drying. A commercial-grade unit typically moves between 1,500 and 3,000 cubic feet per minute (CFM) of air across wet surfaces, dramatically accelerating evaporation from carpet, pad, hardwood, concrete, and drywall.

Placement follows IICRC S500 protocol. The rule of thumb is one air mover per 50–100 square feet of affected area, angled low along baseboards at roughly 45 degrees so the airflow hugs wet wall surfaces rather than bouncing off ceilings uselessly. In a Dunlap finished basement — say, 800 square feet of carpet over a concrete slab — you'd typically see 8–12 air movers running simultaneously.

This is categorically different from anything available at a hardware store. Consumer blower fans typically max out around 200–400 CFM. Running multiple consumer units in a flooded basement for an extended period won't match the performance and documentation that a professional industrial air mover system achieves — and won't produce the systematic documentation your adjuster requires.

💡 Tip: Ask any restoration contractor to show you their air mover placement diagram before they leave after day one. IICRC S500-compliant contractors document placement and CFM readings as part of their drying plan. If a contractor can't produce this, it's a red flag for your insurance documentation.

Dehumidifiers: refrigerant vs. desiccant and when each matters in Central Illinois

An air mover's job is to pull moisture out of wet materials and push it into the air. A dehumidifier's job is to pull that moisture out of the air and exhaust it — either as condensate water or heat. Without dehumidification running in tandem, you're just relocating water vapor, not removing it.

Refrigerant dehumidifiers

Refrigerant dehumidifiers work by drawing humid air across a cold coil. Moisture condenses on the coil and drains away. These units are highly effective in typical interior conditions — rooms above 60°F with moderate humidity loads. A commercial-grade refrigerant dehumidifier typically removes significantly more water per day than residential units available at home improvement stores. For most Category 1 water losses in Peoria — a burst supply line, a dishwasher failure, an overflowing tub — refrigerant dehumidifiers paired with air movers are the standard approach.

Desiccant dehumidifiers

Here's where Central Illinois winters create a specific equipment consideration. Refrigerant dehumidifiers become significantly less effective when ambient temperatures drop below 40–45°F because the cold coil can frost over and stop pulling moisture. In an unheated Peoria garage, a crawl space, or a basement that lost heat during a January pipe burst, refrigerant units underperform — sometimes significantly.

Desiccant dehumidifiers use a silica gel rotor to absorb moisture from air chemically rather than by condensation. They maintain full efficiency at temperatures as low as 20°F, making them the correct tool for the rapid temperature swings that characterize Central Illinois winters — the pattern that causes most of Peoria's burst pipe events each January and February. If your basement lost heat for 48 hours before a pipe burst was discovered, expect desiccant dehumidifiers to be part of the equipment set.

⚠️ Watch out: Some contractors deploy only refrigerant dehumidifiers regardless of ambient temperature because desiccant units are more expensive to operate. If your loss occurred in an unheated or poorly heated space and you're seeing slow moisture readings after 48–72 hours, ask specifically whether desiccant dehumidification has been considered. The IICRC S500 standard requires contractors to select equipment appropriate to job conditions — including temperature.

Moisture meters and thermal imaging: finding water you can't see

Visible water is the easy part. The real damage — and the real liability — is the moisture that wicks into wall cavities, subfloor assemblies, and insulation bays out of sight. This is where Peoria's older housing stock creates compounding risk. A 1940s Peoria Heights bungalow with original plaster-over-lath walls holds and distributes moisture very differently than a 2015 Dunlap subdivision home with fiberglass-batted stud bays. Both require systematic moisture mapping — they just require different equipment settings and expectations.

Penetrating moisture meters

Penetrating meters use two conductive pins driven into wood framing, drywall, or subfloor to measure electrical resistance — which correlates to moisture content. A healthy, dry wood framing member in an equilibrium moisture environment typically reads around 6–10% moisture content. Readings above 16% indicate elevated moisture; readings above 19% are in the range where wood rot and mold growth become active concerns, consistent with EPA mold guidance on moisture thresholds.

Non-penetrating moisture meters

Non-penetrating meters use radio frequency or capacitance technology to detect moisture through surfaces without damaging them — useful for screening large areas quickly, identifying wet zones in drywall without punching pin holes every six inches, and checking hardwood flooring where pin damage is cosmetically unacceptable.

Thermal imaging cameras

Infrared cameras don't directly measure moisture — they measure surface temperature differentials. Wet materials hold temperature differently than dry materials, creating visible cold spots or hot spots on a thermal image that indicate moisture presence behind walls, under flooring, or in ceiling assemblies. Used by trained technicians, thermal imaging can identify wet pockets that neither type of moisture meter would catch without invasive sampling. For Category 2 or Category 3 losses — gray water or sewage backups — thermal imaging is especially critical because contaminated water migrates through concealed cavities.

Negative air machines, HEPA filtration, and specialty systems

Negative air machines

On Category 3 losses — sewage backups, outside floodwater intrusion — standard air movers would spread contaminated particulates throughout the home. Negative air machines create a contained air pressure differential: air flows into the work zone from surrounding clean areas rather than out of it, and that air exhausts through HEPA filtration to the exterior. This is not optional on sewage backup jobs — it's a containment protocol required by IICRC S520 (the mold remediation standard) when mold is present, and by sound Category 3 practice even when visible mold hasn't yet developed.

Sewage backups in Peoria are a known risk factor for homeowners. Older clay tile sewer laterals — common in Peoria Heights and the near North Side — can develop root intrusion and offset joints that cause backups, especially after spring ground thaw. Cleanup costs for Category 3 losses vary significantly based on extent and contamination type. Most standard homeowners policies require a separate sewer backup endorsement for coverage; confirm your endorsement status with your insurer before you need it.

Injectidry and wall cavity drying systems

When water saturates wall cavities — particularly in a Peoria home with fiberglass batt insulation that's become fully saturated — waiting for moisture to migrate out through the drywall surface can take far longer than IICRC S500 drying timelines allow. Injectidry systems drill small ports through baseboard or drywall, inject temperature-controlled, low-humidity air directly into the wall cavity, and exhaust it out another port. This accelerates drying of concealed framing and sheathing without full demolition — and often preserves structural material that might otherwise require tear-out.

The decision to use cavity drying versus perform controlled demolition depends on moisture readings, the category of water, and elapsed time since the loss occurred. Our article on what structural drying is and why it matters covers the factors that drive that decision in more detail.

💡 Tip: All moisture readings taken during drying — including daily logs from air movers, dehumidifier grain readings, and meter readings at each monitoring point — are part of your insurance claim documentation. IICRC S500-compliant contractors produce a drying report at job completion. Request this report explicitly; it's the evidence that justifies the equipment line items on your Xactimate estimate to your adjuster.

Bottom Line

Drying out a Peoria home after water damage isn't a fan-and-wait operation. IICRC S500-compliant drying requires industrial air movers, appropriately selected dehumidifiers (including desiccant units for the cold-space losses that Peoria winters frequently create), thermal imaging, calibrated moisture meters, and — on sewage or contaminated water jobs — negative air containment with HEPA filtration.
