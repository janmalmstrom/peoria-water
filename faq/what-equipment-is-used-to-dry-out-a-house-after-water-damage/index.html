Water Extraction & Structural Drying

What equipment is used to dry out a house after water damage?

By Marcus Webb

Quick Answer:

Professional water damage drying uses air movers, refrigerant or desiccant dehumidifiers, moisture meters, and thermal imaging cameras — all sized and placed to IICRC S500 standards.

Key Takeaways

  • Air movers and dehumidifiers work as a system — air movers alone without dehumidification just move moisture around without removing it from the structure.
  • IICRC S500 requires daily moisture readings to verify drying progress; contractors should provide written moisture logs for your insurance adjuster.
  • Desiccant dehumidifiers outperform refrigerant units in cold conditions — critical for Peoria homes drying in January when interior temps drop after a burst pipe.
  • Thermal imaging cameras find hidden moisture behind walls and under floors without destructive demolition — important in Peoria Heights bungalows where plaster walls complicate drying.

Why does the right drying equipment matter after water damage in a Peoria home?

After water extraction, a structure that looks dry to the eye is rarely dry inside. Water migrates into wall cavities, under flooring, into subfloor assemblies, and through insulation — none of which a shop vac or household fan can reach. The IICRC S500 Standard for Professional Water Damage Restoration establishes equipment selection, placement, and monitoring requirements that professional contractors follow to achieve verified structural drying rather than surface-only drying.

This distinction matters practically in Peoria. A 1940s Peoria Heights bungalow has plaster walls over wood lath, original hardwood floors, and minimal vapor barriers — all of which absorb and retain moisture in ways that modern drywall construction does not. Without the right equipment placed correctly, trapped moisture can become a mold problem within 24–48 hours, per the EPA's guidance on mold growth. Contractors we connect Peoria homeowners with use commercial-grade equipment that is fundamentally different from anything available at a hardware store rental counter.

Core drying equipment used in professional water damage restoration

  • Air movers (axial and centrifugal) — high-velocity fans that accelerate evaporation from wet surfaces
  • Refrigerant dehumidifiers — remove evaporated moisture from the air in normal indoor temperature ranges
  • Desiccant dehumidifiers — function effectively in cold conditions and low-humidity environments
  • Thermal imaging cameras — detect hidden moisture behind walls and under floors without demolition
  • Penetrating and non-penetrating moisture meters — verify moisture content in wood, drywall, and concrete
  • Psychrometers / thermo-hygrometers — measure ambient temperature, relative humidity, and dewpoint
  • Injectidry or wall cavity drying systems — force-dry enclosed wall cavities and subfloor assemblies without full demolition
  • Negative air machines / air scrubbers — filter airborne particles during Category 2 or Category 3 losses

What do air movers actually do, and how many does a job require?

Air movers are the workhorses of structural drying. They direct high-velocity, low-humidity airflow across wet surfaces — floors, walls, and ceilings — to accelerate evaporation. There are two main types used in restoration work: axial fans, which move large volumes of air across open floor areas, and centrifugal (snail shell) air movers, which direct concentrated airflow at wall base angles and under cabinet toe kicks.

Equipment placement follows S500 guidelines based on affected area, material types, and moisture readings — not a flat ratio. A typical industry guideline is roughly one air mover per 50–70 square feet of affected floor space, though actual placement accounts for wall materials, ceiling height, and drying direction. In a finished Dunlap basement with luxury vinyl plank over a concrete slab, placement differs significantly from an unfinished utility room in a Chillicothe home where the concrete foundation is in direct contact with a high groundwater table.

⚠️ Watch out: Air movers running without active dehumidification only redistribute evaporated moisture — they do not remove it. Without dehumidifiers pulling that moisture out of the air, relative humidity rises inside the structure, slowing drying and accelerating mold growth on unaffected materials. Running equipment correctly is as important as running it at all.

What's the difference between refrigerant and desiccant dehumidifiers — and why does it matter in a Peoria winter?

Refrigerant dehumidifiers (also called LGR — Low Grain Refrigerant — units) are the standard for most residential water damage jobs. They work by drawing moist air across a cold coil, condensing water vapor into liquid, and exhausting drier air back into the space. LGR units typically remove 100–200+ pints of water per day under ideal conditions and are the most common unit type on residential losses in Central Illinois.

Desiccant dehumidifiers use a silica-gel rotor to adsorb moisture from the air chemically rather than by condensation. Their critical advantage: they maintain performance in cold temperatures where refrigerant units lose efficiency. This matters directly in Peoria. When a pipe bursts during a "Cat Town Freeze" — the January or February deep-freeze pattern where temperatures drop below zero and then swing 40°F in 24 hours — interior temperatures in an unheated or partially heated home can fall significantly. At 45°F indoor ambient temperature, a refrigerant dehumidifier may be operating at a fraction of its rated capacity. A desiccant unit does not have this limitation.

For large or complex losses — Category 3 sewage backups, multi-floor commercial losses near OSF HealthCare-adjacent medical office buildings, or structure fires with significant water intrusion — contractors may run both types in combination. This is sometimes called a desiccant-refrigerant hybrid drying approach, and it can shorten overall drying time by maintaining grain depression (the difference between the air's current humidity level and its dewpoint) even as temperatures fluctuate.

💡 Tip: Ask the contractor what dehumidifier type they are placing and why. If it's January and they're only using refrigerant units in a cold structure, that's a reasonable question to raise. Local contractors familiar with Central Illinois winters account for this — it's one reason local expertise matters more than franchise brand recognition.

How do thermal imaging cameras and moisture meters fit into the drying process?

Detection equipment is what separates a documented dry-out from a guessed-at one. Two categories are essential: moisture meters (which verify actual moisture content in materials) and thermal imaging cameras (which identify where moisture is hiding before a single piece of drywall is removed).

Moisture meters

Two types are used in combination. Penetrating meters use pins driven into wood or drywall to measure electrical resistance, which correlates to moisture content — the reading is expressed as a percentage. For structural wood, the target dry standard under IICRC S500 is typically below 19% moisture content, though specific targets vary by material and pre-loss conditions. Non-penetrating meters use radio frequency or capacitance to scan surfaces without damage — useful for checking hardwood floors, tile over slab, or plaster walls in older Peoria Heights homes where pin holes cause cosmetic damage.

Daily moisture readings are logged at numbered reference points throughout the affected area. This is a standard documentation practice for insurance purposes. Pekin Insurance adjusters and State Farm field adjusters reviewing Xactimate estimates typically expect to see moisture logs. When logs show consistent progress toward dry standard, the claim moves forward; when drying stalls, it documents why additional steps (demolition, additional equipment) were necessary.

Thermal imaging cameras

Infrared cameras detect temperature differentials on surfaces — wet materials behind walls or under floors show as cooler zones because evaporation is occurring. In a Morton home with a finished basement and LVP flooring, thermal imaging can identify wet subfloor panels without pulling up the entire floor. In a Peoria Heights bungalow with original plaster walls, it can identify which wall cavities hold water without opening every one.

This is relevant to both drying outcomes and insurance documentation. Knowing where moisture actually is — not just where water was visibly running — determines whether wall cavities need drying wands or targeted demolition. It also prevents the opposite mistake: unnecessary demolition of dry areas that drives up claim costs without improving outcomes.

What is an Injectidry system, and when is it used instead of opening walls?

Wall cavity drying systems — the most common brand name is Injectidry — solve a specific problem: water trapped inside a wall assembly that cannot be reached by surface-mounted air movers alone. The system uses a network of small holes drilled at baseboard level into the wall cavity, with hoses connecting to a manifold that pulls air through the cavity using negative pressure. Warm, dry air is circulated through the enclosed space, drawing moisture out through the hose network rather than through demolition.

This approach is appropriate when:

  • Moisture meter and thermal imaging readings confirm wet cavity insulation or framing
  • Wall materials (plaster, tile, high-end cabinetry) make demolition costly or impractical
  • Category 1 water source means the cavity materials are not contaminated and can be dried in place
  • Insurance adjuster has authorized a drying-in-place approach before demolition

Injectidry is not appropriate for Category 3 losses (sewage backup, outside floodwater) where contaminated insulation must be removed regardless of moisture levels — the contamination concern supersedes the drying approach. What monitoring data should a contractor provide — and why does it matter for your insurance claim?

Professional structural drying under IICRC S500 is not a set-it-and-forget-it process. Technicians record daily readings at numbered reference points throughout the affected area — moisture content in wall materials, subfloor, and framing; ambient temperature and relative humidity; and equipment inventory on-site. This data serves two purposes: it confirms the drying is actually progressing, and it provides documentation that insurance adjusters typically need to justify the length and cost of the dry-out.

For Peoria homeowners covered by Country Financial, Pekin Insurance, or State Farm — all carriers with significant market presence in Central Illinois — an adjuster will typically want to see that drying met or approached industry dry standard before authorizing the rebuild phase. Moisture logs with daily readings, thermal images showing before-and-after conditions, and a final clearance report provide that documentation. Contractors we connect you with provide this as part of standard practice, not as an add-on.

If a contractor cannot tell you how they plan to document drying progress or what moisture standard they are targeting, that is a material problem — both for your home's outcome and your insurance claim. Under the Illinois Home Repair and Remodeling Act (HRRA), contractors performing residential work over $1,000 are required to provide a written contract. Ask for written scope and documentation commitments before work begins.

Professional structural drying equipment — air movers, LGR and desiccant dehumidifiers, thermal imaging cameras, moisture meters, and wall cavity drying systems — works as an integrated system governed by IICRC S500 standards.
