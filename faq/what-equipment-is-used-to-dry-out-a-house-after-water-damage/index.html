Water Extraction & Structural Drying

What equipment is used to dry out a house after water damage?

By Marcus Webb

Quick Answer:

Restoration contractors use industrial air movers, refrigerant or desiccant dehumidifiers, moisture meters, thermal imaging cameras, and negative air machines to dry a water-damaged house to IICRC S500 standard.

Key Takeaways

  • Industrial air movers move 150–300 CFM each — roughly 10–20 times the airflow of a household box fan — and are typically placed one per 50–70 square feet of wet flooring.
  • Refrigerant dehumidifiers work best above 70°F; in Peoria's January freeze-thaw events, contractors often switch to desiccant dehumidifiers that function efficiently at near-freezing indoor temperatures.
  • Moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras document drying progress daily — those logs are what insurance adjusters at Pekin Insurance and State Farm use to validate equipment days billed on an Xactimate scope.
  • Negative air machines with HEPA filtration are required for Category 3 (sewage) losses under IICRC S500 to prevent contaminated particulates from spreading through the living space.

Why does professional drying equipment matter — and how is it different from renting a fan?

After a burst pipe or basement flood, the instinct is to grab every box fan in the garage and aim them at the wet carpet. It feels like progress. But standard household fans typically move air at roughly 20–30 cubic feet per minute (CFM). Industrial air movers that restoration contractors deploy typically move 150–300 CFM each — and a properly dried room might have six to ten of them running simultaneously at calculated angles to create a vortex of airflow across wet structural materials.

The difference isn't just speed. Household fans evaporate surface moisture and push it into room air, raising humidity. Without commercial-grade dehumidifiers running in the same space to capture that vapor, you're essentially redistributing moisture through your wall cavities and subfloor rather than removing it. That's the mechanism behind mold growth beginning within 24–48 hours on wet organic materials — a threshold the IICRC S520 standard uses to set remediation timelines.

Professional drying equipment works as a system, not a collection of individual tools. Understanding what each piece does — and why it's on-site — helps you ask the right questions when a contractor walks through your Peoria home after a water loss.

Core Equipment in a Structural Drying System

  • Industrial air movers (axial or centrifugal)
  • Refrigerant dehumidifiers (standard conditions)
  • Desiccant dehumidifiers (low-temperature or high-humidity conditions)
  • Thermal imaging cameras
  • Penetrating and non-penetrating moisture meters
  • Thermo-hygrometers (temperature and relative humidity monitoring)
  • Negative air machines with HEPA filtration (Category 2/3 losses)
  • Injectidry or wall cavity drying systems
  • Drying mats (for hardwood floors)

What do air movers actually do, and how are they positioned?

Air movers are the workhorses of any structural drying project. They fall into two types: axial (propeller-style, high volume, lower pressure — good for large open areas) and centrifugal (snail-shell housing, focused high-velocity airflow — better for directing air under cabinets, beneath floating floors, or along wall bases). Most water damage jobs use centrifugal air movers because they direct airflow precisely where it's needed.

Under IICRC S500 guidelines, placement follows a calculated drying plan based on the affected square footage, material types, and moisture readings. A common starting ratio is typically one air mover per 50–70 square feet of wet floor area, positioned at a low angle (roughly 45 degrees) to push air across the surface of wet materials rather than straight up into room air. The goal is to create a boundary layer of moving air directly at the wet surface — accelerating evaporation from the material itself.

In Peoria Heights, where a significant share of the housing stock consists of 1920s–1940s bungalows with original hardwood floors over plank subfloors, contractors often use drying mats in addition to air movers. Drying mats are vacuum-sealed pads placed directly on hardwood that draw moisture out from below through suction, reducing the chance of cupping or buckling that occurs when hardwood dries unevenly from the top only.

💡 Tip: Ask the contractor for a written drying plan before equipment is placed. IICRC S500 requires a documented drying goal — a target moisture content for each affected material type — before drying begins. That document also becomes part of your insurance claim file.

Refrigerant vs. desiccant dehumidifiers — which is used in Peoria winters?

Refrigerant dehumidifiers (also called LGR — Low Grain Refrigerant — units) are standard for most residential water losses. They work by drawing warm room air over cold refrigerant coils, condensing moisture out of the air, and collecting it in a reservoir or draining it continuously. They're efficient and well-suited to conditions above 60–70°F.

The problem in Central Illinois: Peoria averages more than 20 days per year below freezing, and the freeze-thaw cycle that causes most residential pipe bursts — what we call the "Cat Town Freeze," the January and February deep-freeze pattern — often leaves homes with damaged heating systems or open exterior walls that drop indoor temperatures well below 60°F. Refrigerant dehumidifiers lose significant efficiency below that threshold and can ice up entirely.

Desiccant dehumidifiers use silica gel or similar hygroscopic material to absorb moisture chemically rather than thermally. They operate efficiently at temperatures near or even below freezing — which makes them effective for an unheated Peoria Heights basement in January or a commercial space in East Peoria where a broken pipe has taken out the HVAC system. Experienced local contractors typically carry both types and select based on the conditions they find on-site, not what's convenient to load on a truck.

How do moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras document the drying process?

Equipment that extracts water is half the job. Equipment that measures water — and proves it's gone — is what determines whether your insurance claim closes cleanly or gets disputed.

Penetrating moisture meters use metal pins driven into wood framing, subfloor, or drywall to measure electrical resistance, which correlates to moisture content. A properly dried wood subfloor typically should read at or near its pre-loss equilibrium moisture content (EMC) — generally 7–12% in Central Illinois homes, depending on season. Readings above that threshold mean drying isn't complete, regardless of how dry the surface feels.

Non-penetrating moisture meters use radio frequency or electromagnetic signals to detect moisture in materials without leaving pin holes — useful for tile, concrete, and finished surfaces where you want a quick assessment before deciding whether to probe deeper.

Thermal imaging cameras (infrared cameras) detect temperature differences in building materials. Wet materials hold temperature differently than dry ones — appearing cooler on an IR image when evaporation is occurring, or warmer when water is trapped behind a finished surface. Thermal imaging identifies wet areas hidden behind drywall or under tile without destructive investigation, which reduces unnecessary demolition and gives adjusters a visual record of moisture migration.

Thermo-hygrometers measure the temperature and relative humidity of room air simultaneously. Under IICRC S500, contractors record these readings daily alongside moisture meter readings to create a daily drying log. That log is the primary document adjusters review when evaluating equipment days on an Xactimate estimate. A contractor who can't produce daily moisture logs has a claim file that's easy to dispute.

⚠️ Watch out: If a contractor removes equipment and declares the job "dry" without providing written final moisture readings at or below the documented drying goal, request those readings before signing any completion paperwork. Verbal confirmation of dryness isn't the same as documented proof — and it won't help you if mold develops three weeks later and an adjuster asks when the structure was certified dry.

What specialized equipment is used for wall cavities, sewage backups, and hard-to-reach areas?

Visible wet carpet and standing water are straightforward. The harder problems are the ones you can't see: moisture wicked into wall cavities, wet insulation inside partition walls, or contamination from a sewage backup that has soaked into framing behind the drywall.

Injectidry systems — sometimes called wall cavity drying systems — drill small holes in drywall at baseboard level, then inject high-velocity dry air directly into the wall cavity. The air circulates through the stud bay and exits through another port, carrying moisture with it. This technique preserves finished walls that would otherwise require full demotion to dry the framing behind them. In older Peoria homes with blown-in insulation or original lath-and-plaster walls, contractors assess whether cavity drying is viable or whether the insulation retains too much water to dry in place.

Negative air machines with HEPA filtration are required on Category 3 losses — sewage backups, toilet overflows, or any water that originated from outside the building — under IICRC S500 protocols. These machines create negative air pressure in the contaminated zone, preventing airborne contaminants and particulates from migrating to unaffected areas of the home. They vent filtered air either outside through a window or through sequential HEPA filters. Homeowners dealing with sewage backup cleanup — a common call in older neighborhoods of Peoria Heights and Chillicothe where clay sewer laterals are aging — should expect to see negative air machines as standard equipment on Category 3 losses.

What should I expect to see on a contractor's equipment list for my Peoria home?

When a local restoration contractor responds to your water loss, their scope of work — the document they submit to your insurance adjuster — will itemize equipment by type, quantity, and number of days deployed. Xactimate, the estimating software most Illinois adjusters use, has line items for each equipment category. Knowing what's on that list helps you understand what you're paying for, or what your insurer is approving.

A typical residential water loss in a Peoria home — for example, a burst supply line in a Dunlap finished basement with 800 square feet of affected area — might typically include:

  • 8–12 centrifugal air movers running for typically 3–5 days (Category 1 loss, clean water from a supply pipe)
  • 2–3 LGR refrigerant dehumidifiers (or desiccant units if it's January and the basement is cold)
  • 1 negative air machine if any Category 2 conditions are present (water near HVAC, dishwasher, etc.)
  • Moisture meter readings at 15–20 monitoring points, logged daily
  • Thermal imaging scan at initial assessment and at dry-out completion
  • Injectidry system if finished walls show elevated moisture readings that don't respond to surface drying

A Category 3 sewage backup — the most equipment-intensive scenario — adds negative air machines, air scrubbers, and often extends drying to 7–10 days depending on material contamination. Costs for sewage cleanup in the Peoria market typically range from $5,000–$12,000 compared to $2,800–$6,500 for a standard residential water loss. Note that sewer backup coverage requires a separate endorsement on most homeowners policies — confirm that coverage with your adjuster before assuming it applies.

💡 Tip: Under the Illinois Home Repair and Remodeling Act (HRRA), any residential contractor performing work over $1,000 must provide a written contract before work begins. That contract should reference the equipment to be deployed and the drying standard being targeted. Request it before equipment is placed — not after.
Bottom Line

Professional water damage drying in a Peoria home requires a coordinated system of industrial air movers, correctly matched dehumidifiers (refrigerant for warm conditions, desiccant for the cold January pipe-burst events common in Central Illinois), and precision measurement tools — moisture meters, thermal imaging cameras, and daily drying logs. That documentation isn't bureaucratic overhead; it's what allows local adjusters to approve equipment days on your claim. Contact a local Peoria contractor who carries the full equipment inventory and documents every reading to IICRC S500 standard.
