Water Extraction & Structural Drying

What equipment is used to dry out a house after water damage?

By Marcus Webb

Quick Answer:

Professional water damage drying uses industrial dehumidifiers, air movers, moisture meters, and thermal imaging cameras working together to remove water from structure and air to IICRC S500 standards.

Key Takeaways

  • Industrial desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers remove 30–100+ pints of moisture per day from air — far beyond what a hardware store rental unit can achieve.
  • Air movers are positioned at specific angles along baseboards to create a vortex drying effect on walls and subfloor, not just to circulate air generally.
  • Restoration contractors use penetrating and non-penetrating moisture meters to track drying progress in wood framing and drywall — daily logs are required under IICRC S500 and matter when filing with insurers like Pekin Insurance or State Farm.
  • Thermal imaging cameras detect hidden moisture pockets behind walls and under floors that would otherwise cause mold growth within 24–48 hours on wet organic material.

What types of dehumidifiers are used in professional water damage restoration?

Professional restoration contractors use two classes of industrial dehumidifiers — refrigerant (LGR) and desiccant — that operate at a significantly larger scale than anything available at a hardware store. A low-grain refrigerant (LGR) dehumidifier typically removes 80–150 pints of moisture per day from the air in a structure; a standard consumer unit typically pulls 30–50 pints under ideal conditions, and performance drops sharply in the colder indoor temperatures common to Peoria homes during January pipe bursts. Desiccant dehumidifiers use silica-based material to absorb moisture chemically rather than by cooling air, which makes them especially effective when temperatures inside a damaged structure are below 50°F — a realistic scenario in a Peoria home where heat has been disrupted after a pipe failure in mid-winter.

The number and placement of dehumidifiers is calculated using IICRC S500 psychrometric formulas based on the volume of affected space, measured moisture levels in materials, and the water category involved. This protocol ensures professional drying produces measurable, documentable results that support claims with insurance adjusters.

Dehumidifier Types Used in Professional Structural Drying

  • LGR Refrigerant Dehumidifiers: Typically 80–150 pints/day; most common for residential losses; best above 50°F
  • Desiccant Dehumidifiers: Chemical absorption; effective in cold temps; often used in Peoria winter losses where interior temps are depressed
  • Consumer/Rental Units: Typically 30–50 pints/day under ideal conditions; not rated for restoration use; not compliant with IICRC S500 drying protocols

How do air movers accelerate drying in a water-damaged home?

Air movers — often called axial fans or centrifugal blowers — work by stripping the thin boundary layer of saturated air sitting directly against wet surfaces like drywall, hardwood, and subfloor, replacing it with drier ambient air so evaporation can continue at maximum speed. Without this constant air exchange, moisture migrates deeper into structural materials rather than evaporating out, turning a contained Category 1 cleanup into a weeks-long drying project. In practice, contractors place air movers at a low angle (typically 45 degrees) along baseboard level to push air up and across wall cavities, creating a circular flow that simultaneously dries the floor assembly below and the wall framing above.

IICRC S500 specifies equipment placement ratios — typically one air mover per 50–70 square feet of affected area — to ensure complete coverage. In a finished Dunlap basement with engineered hardwood over a concrete slab, air mover placement requires additional consideration for the air gap between flooring layers. A contractor unfamiliar with Central Illinois construction norms — finished basements are nearly universal in Dunlap's newer subdivisions — may underplace equipment and miss the subfloor layer entirely.

💡 Tip: Ask the restoration contractor to show you the equipment placement log and daily psychrometric readings. Under IICRC S500, these records document that drying reached the target moisture content — and they're exactly what insurance adjusters need to close your claim without dispute.

What do moisture meters and thermal cameras actually measure?

Moisture meters give restoration contractors the numerical data needed to confirm a structure is dry — not just dry to the touch, but dry to the wood moisture content baseline required by IICRC S500. Two types are used together: pin-type (penetrating) meters that measure moisture content in wood framing and subfloor by driving small probes into the material, and non-penetrating (inductive) meters that detect moisture through finished surfaces like drywall or hardwood without causing additional damage. A baseline "dry standard" for comparable unaffected materials in the same home is established on day one, and daily readings must show a consistent downward trend toward that baseline.

Thermal imaging cameras (infrared cameras) detect temperature differences in surfaces — wet materials retain heat differently than dry materials, showing up as distinct color signatures on the camera screen. This matters enormously in Peoria Heights, where the 1920s–1940s bungalow stock often has original plaster walls over wood lath. A pipe failure behind plaster can saturate the cavity without any visible surface staining. Without thermal imaging, that moisture pocket goes undetected, warms up as spring approaches, and creates conditions where mold can begin growing within 24–48 hours on wet organic material — exactly what the EPA mold guidance identifies as the critical intervention window.

⚠️ Watch out: Thermal imaging identifies suspect areas, but it does not directly measure moisture content. A legitimate restoration contractor uses both thermal cameras and moisture meters together — thermal to locate, meters to quantify. A contractor who relies on visual inspection alone is not following IICRC S500 protocol.

What specialized equipment is used for sewage backups or contaminated water?

Category 3 water losses — sewage backups, toilet overflows involving fecal matter, or water that has contacted outside floodwater — require containment and air scrubbing equipment that isn't needed for clean water (Category 1) drying jobs. Specifically, contractors bring HEPA air scrubbers, which filter airborne contaminants and bioaerosols from the work area, and hydroxyl generators or ozone generators for odor control after sanitization. Negative air pressure containment may also be established to prevent cross-contamination from the affected area to the rest of the home.

This distinction matters in Peoria because sewage backup cleanup typically ranges from several thousand to over ten thousand dollars locally and requires a separate policy endorsement on most standard homeowners policies — it isn't automatically covered the way sudden pipe bursts are. The equipment required for Category 3 work is also more extensive, and the documentation of contamination category, containment procedures, and air quality readings is critical to getting a claim approved. Why does the right equipment matter for your insurance claim?

Insurance documentation for water damage claims — particularly with adjusters working off Xactimate pricing — requires line-item equipment records, not just a final bill. Each dehumidifier, air mover, and air scrubber placed generates a daily equipment charge that adjusters at major carriers recognize and approve when documented against IICRC S500 protocols. A contractor who under-deploys equipment to cut costs, or who can't produce daily moisture logs showing progress toward the dry standard, leaves you in a position where the adjuster may question whether the work was complete.

This is particularly relevant in the Peoria market, where local adjusters are familiar with regional restoration pricing and local building stock. A claim with proper equipment documentation, daily psychrometric readings, and a clear drying log closes faster and with fewer disputes than one supported only by a final invoice. For technical standards governing this equipment use, the IICRC S500 standard is the governing document.

Drying a water-damaged Peoria home to IICRC S500 standard requires industrial LGR or desiccant dehumidifiers, axial air movers placed at specific ratios, penetrating and non-penetrating moisture meters, and thermal imaging cameras working as a system — not a single rental unit from a hardware store. In Central Illinois, where January pipe bursts happen in already-cold structures and finished basements in Dunlap or Morton can have multiple material layers to dry, equipment selection and placement directly affect whether your structure dries completely and whether your claim documentation holds up with your insurer. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I rent a dehumidifier from Home Depot instead of hiring a professional?

Consumer rental dehumidifiers typically pull 30–50 pints per day under ideal conditions, while the industrial LGR units used in professional restoration remove 80–150 pints per day. More critically, consumer units don't come with the moisture meters, thermal cameras, or daily documentation that insurers like State Farm and Pekin Insurance require to validate a structural drying claim — so you may end up paying out of pocket for a job that could have been covered.

How many air movers does a professional use for a typical Peoria basement flood?

IICRC S500 protocols call for approximately one air mover per 50–70 square feet of affected area, so a 600-square-foot flooded basement typically requires 9–12 units placed at specific angles along the perimeter. Finished basements — common throughout Dunlap and Morton subdivisions — may require additional units to address the air gap between a floating floor and the concrete slab below.

What is a moisture meter and why does it matter for my insurance claim?

A moisture meter measures the actual water content in structural materials like wood framing, subfloor, and drywall as a percentage, allowing contractors to document a measurable decline toward the dry standard established by IICRC S500. Daily moisture meter readings create the paper trail that insurance adjusters at carriers like Country Financial and USAA use to confirm the drying work was completed properly before authorizing reconstruction costs.

Do contractors use different equipment for sewage backups than for burst pipe flooding?

Yes — sewage backups are classified as Category 3 (black water) under IICRC S500 and require HEPA air scrubbers to capture airborne bioaerosols, negative air pressure containment to prevent cross-contamination, and often hydroxyl or ozone generators for odor control after sanitization. These requirements don't apply to Category 1 (clean water) burst pipe losses, which is part of why sewage cleanup costs in the Peoria market typically run $5,000–$12,000 compared to $2,800–$6,500 for standard residential water damage.

What does thermal imaging show that a moisture meter doesn't?

A thermal (infrared) camera reveals temperature differentials across large surface areas quickly, flagging suspect zones where wet material may be hiding behind plaster or drywall — common in Peoria Heights homes with original 1920s–1940s plaster-over-lath construction. The moisture meter then quantifies the actual moisture content in those flagged areas; thermal imaging locates, meters measure, and both together are required under IICRC S500 for a defensible drying assessment.

Why do contractors use desiccant dehumidifiers instead of standard units in Peoria winters?

Refrigerant-based dehumidifiers lose significant efficiency when indoor temperatures drop below 50°F, which can happen in Peoria homes where heating has been disrupted by a pipe burst during a deep-freeze event in January or February. Desiccant dehumidifiers use chemical absorption rather than refrigeration coils, maintaining effective moisture removal even in cold structures — making them the appropriate choice for the "Cat Town Freeze" pipe burst pattern Central Illinois sees every winter.

How do I know the contractor is using enough equipment?

Ask for the equipment placement log, which a contractor following IICRC S500 protocol will produce on day one. It should list the make, model, and placement location of every dehumidifier and air mover, along with the calculation basis (square footage, material type, water category) that determined the quantity. Under Illinois HRRA requirements, the written contract for any job over $1,000 should also specify the equipment scope — this protects you if disputes arise later.

Does my homeowners insurance cover the cost of this drying equipment?

Sudden and accidental water damage — like a burst pipe — is typically covered by standard homeowners policies, including the cost of professional drying equipment deployed according to IICRC S500 standards. The daily equipment charges for dehumidifiers and air movers are line items adjusters recognize in Xactimate estimating software, and proper documentation makes approval more straightforward. Sewage backup and exterior floodwater are separate coverage questions that depend on your specific policy endorsements and, in flood-zone areas near the Illinois River, NFIP flood insurance.
