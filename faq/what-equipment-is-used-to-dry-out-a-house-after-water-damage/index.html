Water Extraction & Structural Drying

What equipment is used to dry out a house after water damage?

By Marcus Webb

Quick Answer:

Professional water damage drying uses commercial dehumidifiers, air movers, moisture meters, and thermal imaging cameras working together as a system — not just fans.

Key Takeaways

  • Commercial-grade desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers remove 10–30x more moisture per day than any household unit you can buy at a hardware store.
  • Air movers (axial and centrifugal) create high-velocity airflow across wet surfaces, accelerating evaporation into the air where dehumidifiers capture it — they must be used together or neither works efficiently.
  • Moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras are how technicians verify drying is complete to IICRC S500 standard — without them, hidden moisture in wall cavities causes mold within 24–48 hours.
  • In Peoria's January–February freeze season, restoration contractors must balance ventilation strategy differently than in summer — outdoor air is too cold to assist drying and can cause condensation on interior surfaces.

Why household fans and dehumidifiers aren't enough after water damage

The first instinct for most Peoria homeowners after a burst pipe or basement flood is to grab a box fan and a dehumidifier from Menards. It's a reasonable reaction — you want to do something. The problem is that consumer-grade equipment isn't designed for structural drying. A standard 70-pint household dehumidifier typically removes roughly 70 pints (about 8.75 gallons) of moisture per day under ideal conditions. A commercial LGR (Low Grain Refrigerant) dehumidifier used by restoration contractors typically removes 100–200+ pints per day — and performs far better in the low-humidity conditions that exist once a space starts to dry out, which is exactly when consumer units lose efficiency.

Water doesn't stay on the surface of your floor. After a significant water event, moisture migrates into wall cavities, subfloor assemblies, insulation, and framing — sometimes within hours. The IICRC S500 Standard for Professional Water Damage Restoration defines the equipment requirements, documentation protocols, and drying goals that separate a professional dry-out from one that sets you up for a mold problem six weeks later.

For Peoria homes built before 1980 — which make up a substantial portion of housing stock in neighborhoods like Peoria Heights and the Near North Side — this matters even more. Older balloon-frame construction allows water to travel vertically through wall cavities faster than in modern platform-frame homes, and galvanized or cast-iron drain lines corrode faster when chronically wet.

⚠️ Watch out: Running air movers without dehumidifiers — or dehumidifiers without air movers — is a common DIY mistake. Air movers accelerate evaporation from surfaces into the air; dehumidifiers extract that moisture from the air. Without both working together, you're either not evaporating fast enough or pushing humid air into unaffected areas of the house.

The core drying equipment: what restoration contractors bring on-site

Commercial dehumidifiers

There are two main types used in structural drying:

  • LGR (Low Grain Refrigerant) dehumidifiers: The workhorse of most residential water damage jobs. These refrigerant-based units cool incoming air below its dew point to condense moisture, then reheat the air before returning it to the space. They're effective in the moderate temperature ranges typical of a Peoria basement or main floor and can process several hundred cubic feet of air per minute.
  • Desiccant dehumidifiers: Use silica gel or similar material to adsorb moisture from air chemically rather than thermally. These perform better in colder conditions — which matters significantly in Peoria during a January pipe burst when a basement may be 40°F or lower. Refrigerant dehumidifiers typically lose efficiency below 60°F; desiccant units maintain performance down to near-freezing. Contractors doing winter restoration work in Central Illinois often deploy desiccant units specifically for this reason.

The number of dehumidifiers placed on a job is calculated based on the volume of wet air space, the class of water damage (per IICRC S500: Class 1 through Class 4, based on how much material is affected and how difficult it is to dry), and moisture readings at intake. This is not guesswork — it's a calculation.

Air movers

Air movers are high-velocity, low-profile fans specifically engineered to direct concentrated airflow across wet structural surfaces — floors, walls, and ceilings. Two main types are used:

  • Axial air movers: Move large volumes of air at lower pressure. Good for open spaces and general airflow across large floor areas.
  • Centrifugal (snail) air movers: Move air at higher pressure through a smaller outlet. Better for directing airflow under cabinets, behind walls through drilled access holes, and into tight cavities where axial units can't reach.

On a typical residential job, contractors generally position one air mover per 10–16 linear feet of affected wall, adjusted for the drying class. They're placed at a low angle to create a vortex effect across the floor and up the wall surface — not just blowing air randomly into the room.

Drying mats and floor drying systems

For water-damaged hardwood floors — common in older Peoria homes and in finished basements throughout Dunlap and Morton — contractors use specialized floor drying mat systems. These create a negative pressure zone directly beneath the flooring surface, pulling moisture up through the wood from below rather than just evaporating from the top. This approach may help preserve hardwood floors that would otherwise require full replacement, which represents a significant cost difference for homeowners working with a State Farm or Country Financial adjuster.

Injectidry and wall cavity drying systems

When moisture meters confirm water has entered wall cavities — which is common in Category 2 (gray water) losses from appliance failures and in older homes with less vapor barrier protection — contractors drill small access holes and inject filtered, dehumidified air directly into the cavity using specialized hose systems. This avoids the cost of complete wall demolition in cases where the framing itself isn't saturated beyond recovery. The decision to inject versus demolish is driven by moisture readings, not guesswork.

💡 Tip: Ask any restoration contractor what drying class they've assigned your job per IICRC S500. If they can't answer — or don't know what that means — that's a significant red flag. Class assignment drives equipment quantity, placement, and expected drying timeline.

Detection and monitoring equipment: how technicians know when it's actually dry

The drying equipment above does the work. The detection equipment below tells contractors whether that work is done. This distinction matters enormously — completing drying to a documented standard is what protects you against mold and what insurance adjusters need to close a claim properly.

Moisture meters

Two types are used in combination:

  • Pin-type moisture meters: Drive small metal pins into wood, drywall, or other materials and measure electrical resistance to determine moisture content. Accurate and direct, but leaves small holes. Used to establish baseline readings at the start of a job and verify final drying targets.
  • Non-penetrating (pinless) moisture meters: Use radio frequency or electromagnetic signals to detect moisture in materials without puncturing the surface. Faster for scanning large areas, though calibration by material type matters. Used for initial mapping and ongoing monitoring between pin readings.

Under IICRC S500, technicians are required to establish drying goals — specific target moisture content readings for each material type — and document daily readings until those goals are reached. This daily moisture log is also the documentation your adjuster needs to verify the scope of work performed.

Thermal imaging cameras (infrared cameras)

Thermal cameras detect temperature differentials on surfaces — and wet materials read differently than dry materials because evaporative cooling lowers surface temperature. A thermal scan can reveal moisture hidden behind drywall, under flooring, or above ceiling tiles without destructive investigation. This is particularly useful in Peoria Heights bungalows and other older homes with plaster walls, where determining the extent of water migration would otherwise require significant demolition.

Thermal imaging is a screening tool, not a standalone measurement. Any suspect area identified by thermal imaging must be confirmed with a moisture meter reading — cameras show temperature, not moisture content directly. Contractors who use thermal imaging only, without following up with meters, are cutting corners.

Thermo-hygrometers and psychrometric calculations

Thermo-hygrometers measure temperature and relative humidity in the drying environment. Contractors use these readings alongside psychrometric charts (or software) to calculate the specific humidity (grains per pound) of the air — which tells them whether the drying system is performing as expected and whether dehumidifier settings or placement need adjustment. A drying chamber that isn't trending toward lower specific humidity after 24 hours is a sign something is wrong: equipment undersized, an undetected moisture source still active, or inadequate air movement.

How Peoria's winters change the equipment strategy

Most water damage content online assumes a temperate environment. Peoria's freeze-thaw cycle creates specific challenges that affect equipment selection and placement strategy.

During the peak burst pipe season — January and February, when temperatures can swing from -5°F to 35°F within 24 hours — basements and crawl spaces may be 35–45°F when contractors arrive. At those temperatures, standard LGR refrigerant dehumidifiers are typically significantly less efficient. Restoration contractors working winter jobs in Central Illinois often supplement with desiccant dehumidifiers or use temporary heat sources (propane heaters, electric forced-air units) to bring the space temperature up to 70°F+ before deploying refrigerant units. The heat also accelerates evaporation from structural materials.

Ventilating with outdoor air — a common drying assist technique in summer months — is generally counterproductive in January. Cold outdoor air in Peoria carries very little moisture capacity; introducing it to a warm interior space causes condensation on cold surfaces rather than assisting drying. Contractors typically adjust the strategy to a closed-system approach: recirculating and dehumidifying interior air rather than exchanging it with outdoor air.

This matters if you're comparing quotes. A contractor who treats a January pipe burst in a Dunlap finished basement the same way they'd treat a July appliance leak in an East Peoria ranch home is not applying the S500 standard correctly.

Water damage drying equipment: what to expect on a professional job

  • LGR dehumidifiers — commercial units typically removing 100–200+ pints/day; quantity calculated by drying class
  • Desiccant dehumidifiers — used when space temps are below 60°F (critical in Peoria winters)
  • Axial and centrifugal air movers — high-velocity fans positioned per IICRC S500 placement ratios, not guesswork
  • Floor drying mat systems — negative-pressure drying for hardwood and engineered wood floors
  • Injectidry wall cavity systems — targeted drying inside walls without full demolition
  • Pin and pinless moisture meters — daily readings that document drying progress to insurer standards
  • Thermal imaging cameras — screen for hidden moisture; always confirmed by meter
  • Thermo-hygrometers — monitor ambient conditions and verify psychrometric performance

How equipment documentation affects your insurance claim

The equipment list above isn't just about getting your house dry — it's about creating a defensible record for your insurance adjuster. Restoration contractors connected through our network use daily moisture logs, psychrometric data, and equipment placement documentation that aligns with Xactimate line items, which is the estimating software most major carriers — including State Farm, Pekin Insurance, Allstate, and Country Financial — use to evaluate claims.

When a Pekin Insurance adjuster in Tazewell County reviews a claim, they're looking for documented drying goals, equipment counts, and daily moisture readings. A restoration job without this documentation leaves your adjuster without the evidence to approve the full scope of work. Contractors who skip the paperwork aren't doing you a favor — they're creating a gap that can reduce your claim payout or result in disputed line items.

Mold is the downstream consequence of inadequate or undocumented drying. The EPA notes that mold can typically begin growing within 24–48 hours on wet organic materials — a threshold also recognized by the IICRC S520 mold remediation standard. If a restoration contractor dries your structure to a documented standard, you have evidence that mold remediation was not warranted. If they don't, you may face a mold dispute with your insurer months later when remediation costs are no longer part of the original claim.

In Central Illinois winters, desiccant dehumidifiers and closed-system drying strategy are often required that most out-of-state storm chasers don't plan for.
